# Question toolbox

If a question has an obvious right answer, candidates will give it. Use techniques that make the honest answer the easy one.

## Contents
1. Techniques
2. Rules for using them
3. The "I don't know" recovery ladder

5. Common red flags

## 1. Techniques

| Technique | How it works | Example | What it reveals |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Timeline pinning | Force specifics: who, when, what was said | "Who knew about the error by end of day? By end of week?" | Inflated or invented stories fall apart on dates and names |
| Cost pinning | Ask what doing the right thing cost them | "What did it cost you, and who was upset?" | Courage is only real if something was at stake |
| Reverse inquiry | Ask about failure, quitting or sabotage | "If you wanted this project to fail without being blamed, how?" | Real mental models of what goes wrong |
| Projective | Ask about "other people" | "Why do good engineers stay quiet when they see a problem?" | Beliefs people filter when talking about themselves |
| Bracketing | Pin between two extremes; justify both sides | "What percent was you? Why not 10 higher? 10 lower?" | Calibration; kills vague self-ratings |
| False-premise trap | State something flawed, confidently | "Metrics jumped after launch, so it worked, right?" | Whether they agree to please or hold a standard |
| Withheld information | Leave a key fact out of a problem | Omit a delay or constraint; see if they ask | Curiosity; whether they assume or ask |
| Planted error | Make a plausible mistake in front of them | A sign flip on a shared whiteboard | Whether and how kindly they correct a peer |
| Role-play | Live pressure from a realistic counterpart | A partner team demands the system be switched off | Holding a standard while keeping the relationship |
| Decision drill | On their own project, skip the story, ask why | "Why that approach? What did you try first that failed?" | Real ownership and depth on home turf |
| Live tool review | Show flawed output from a tool (e.g. AI) and ask "would you use it?" | An AI-generated answer with one subtle flaw | Tool fluency plus critical judgment |

## 2. Rules for using them

- **Three levels deep.** "What did you say exactly?" → "What did they say back?" → "What happened next?" The first answer is rehearsed; the third is real.
- **Stay neutral.** No nods, smiles or "great answer"; they telegraph the right answer, more so on video.
- **Wait after traps.** Five seconds of silence; people fill it with what they really think.
- **Traps sparingly.** One or two per session. Never reveal one they fell for; acknowledge one they caught.
- **Pick the project yourself** for decision drills, so they can't choose their best-rehearsed story.
- **Pair stated with observed.** E.g. ask about holding a standard (stated), then run a trap or role-play (observed); compare in the debrief.

## 3. The "I don't know" recovery ladder

Tell candidates upfront that "I don't know" is fine. When it happens, run these in order, stopping once they get traction:

1. "What's your best guess, and why?"
2. "What's the closest thing you've done, and how would it transfer?"
3. "What would you ask, or look up, to find out?"

Cap at about 90 seconds to 2 minutes, then give the answer kindly and move on. Score the reasoning, not the missing fact. A confident bluff is a red flag (transparency). Whether they keep reasoning or give up is also a grit read. If most candidates say "I don't know" to the same question, the question is miscalibrated: add context or replace it.

## 4. Example probes by trait

For each: the question, follow-ups, then strong / weak.

### Transparency
- **Timeline pinning:** "Walk me through the last time you found an error in your own work." → "Who knew by end of day? By end of week?" → "Did anyone find out who would never have found it themselves?" Strong: names, same-day, unprompted. Weak: "I fixed it before anyone was affected."
- **Projective:** "When people hide a problem with their work, why do you think they do it?" Strong: names systemic causes and how they've resisted them. Weak: sympathetic to hiding.
- **Trap:** "Here we bring solutions, not problems; don't escalate until it's fixed." Strong: pushes back. Weak: agrees.

### High agency / self-optimization
- "Tell me about something in your own work you improved or automated that nobody asked you to." → "What changed? Did anyone adopt it?" Strong: specific, recent, measurable. Weak: only assigned work.
- **Reverse:** "What was broken at your last job for a long time and never got fixed? Why?" Strong: tried something, or honest about why not. Weak: blames others, never involved.
- **Projective:** "Why do some people end up just maintaining things for years?"

### Courage (standard over being liked)
- **Cost pinning:** "Tell me about a time doing the right thing cost you something. What did it cost, and who was upset? Would you do it again?" Strong: a real cost, would repeat. Weak: no cost, or everyone already agreed.
- **Projective:** "What would a partner you worked with say is your most annoying habit?" Strong: a real habit rooted in a standard. Weak: disguised strengths.
- **Bracketing:** "A time you backed down, and a time you held firm. Was each right?" Strong: distinguishes persuasion by evidence from pressure.
- **Observed:** false-premise traps; a role-play where a partner escalates emotionally, then by authority.

### Grit
- "Tell me about the longest you've stuck with a problem that didn't want to be solved. When did you almost quit, and what kept you going?" Strong: specific low point and what they changed. Weak: no low point.
- **Reverse:** "What's something you gave up on that you still think about? Was quitting right?" Strong: knows a dead end from a hard problem.
- **Observed:** persistence through the recovery ladder when stuck.

### Native use of new technology (e.g. AI)
- **Timeline pinning:** "The last three things you used AI for this week, one from outside work." Native: instant, mundane, specific. Not native: generic or long pause.
- "Where has it been confidently wrong for you, and how did you catch it?" Native: specific failure modes and verification habits.
- **Reverse:** "What have you stopped using it for? What's changed in how you work in six months?"
- **Observed:** live review of AI output with one subtle flaw. Strong: finds the flaw, says how they'd verify, still sees value. Weak: accepts wholesale or dismisses outright.

### Helping others be better
- "Who got better because of you? What would *they* say you did? What were they better at than you?" Strong: specific, generous credit. Weak: can't name anyone.
- "A teammate you thought was underperforming: what did you do first?" Strong: spoke to them directly. Weak: complained or escalated first.
- **Observed:** planted error. Strong: flags it kindly, explains, preserves dignity.

### Inquisitiveness
- **Observed:** withheld information; count and depth of candidate questions every round.
- "What's something about a system you worked on that you still don't understand?"

### Strategic problem solving
- "You can fund one of A, B or C. Which, and why?" → push back once → "What data would change your mind?" Strong: clear priority, updates with reasons. Weak: "it depends" with no choice, or defends regardless.
- **Reverse:** "Where would you never use [popular approach]?"

### Driving programs
- "Your hardest program: what did you drop to make it happen, and who was unhappy?" Pin when it slipped and who escalated.
- **Bracketing:** "What percent of the outcome was you? Why not 10 higher or lower?"

### Retention (motivation match)
- "What are you hoping to do more of in your next role? Less of?" Compare honestly with what the role offers.
- "Why did you leave?" at each move, pinned to timelines. Look for moves toward something vs. repeatedly away from something; ask before assuming.

### Potential (learning velocity, coachability)
- "Something you learned in the last six months that changed how you work. How did you learn it?"
- "How is this like or unlike things you've done before?" (transfer)
- "Where do you want to be in three years, and what are you doing about it now?"
- **Observed:** give one hint mid-problem; do they integrate and build on it, or ignore or defend?

## 5. Common red flags
- Blames partners or "the organization" for failures.
- Universal answers ("always use X" / "never use X") without conditions.
- Asks no questions across rounds.
- Can't describe their own mistakes concretely.
- Details get vaguer the deeper you go; "the team" made every decision.
- Agrees with a confident wrong claim; bluffs instead of "I don't know."

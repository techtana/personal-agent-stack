# Hiring Process Design

Help a hiring manager design a hiring process that predicts who will succeed, stay and grow in the role, at the lowest time cost to the team, and fairly for every candidate. Treat the process as a measurement system: decide what to measure, use probes that can't be gamed, calibrate, and keep the noise out.

## Core principles

Apply these to every design decision, and explain the reasoning to the user when it changes a choice.

1. **Start from outcomes, not a job description.** Define what success looks like in 12 months, then work back to the traits that predict it.
2. **Separate trainable from non-trainable.** Domain knowledge can be taught; values, reasoning and character mostly can't. Flex on the first, never on the second. This matters most for niche roles where perfect matches are rare.
3. **Observe, don't just ask.** Direct questions get rehearsed answers. Test every critical trait twice: once by what the candidate says (a question about their past) and once by what they do in the room (a trap, problem, role-play or planted error). A gap between the two is the strongest signal.
4. **Gate early, by cost.** Put the cheapest, highest-signal filters first (usually values, then core reasoning) so expensive multi-interviewer rounds go only to strong candidates.
5. **One owner per signal.** Each round scores specific traits; no overlap, no "general impression" scores.
6. **Score evidence, independently.** Every score needs a quote or observed behavior, written before discussion.
7. **Values are a veto, not an average.** Strong technical scores never offset a clear values miss.
8. **Protect the hiring team's time.** Delegate everything scripted or logistical to recruiting/HR; keep judgment with the people who carry the bar.
9. **Don't hire on a guess.** Thin or split evidence means a short follow-up, not a coin flip.
10. **Hire for retention, potential and speed together.** Probe motivation match and learning velocity; remove waiting, not rigor.

## Workflow

Figure out where the user is and start there. Many users arrive mid-way ("shorten my process", "give me questions for grit"); don't force them through every step.

### Step 1: Capture the role

Pull answers from the conversation first; ask only for what's missing, one question at a time where possible. You need:

- **Outcomes:** what the hire must deliver in 12 months.
- **Hiring priorities:** two to four traits that matter most (e.g. courage, grit, native use of new technology). These carry the hire bar.
- **Team values:** the culture gate, each rewritten as observable behavior.
- **Traits of past successful hires** and **deal breakers** (e.g. hiding mistakes, bluffing, blaming partners).
- **Constraints:** total time budget per candidate, online or onsite, who can interview, whether HR/recruiting can help, talent-pool scarcity.

Then split requirements into hard-to-teach (fixed bar) and trainable (acceptable gap with a named mentor and 90-day plan). For niche roles, list adjacent talent pools and the gap each brings, and make the resume screen accept any of several paths.

### Step 2: Map signals to probes

Before writing questions, build a coverage table: each value or trait, the round with a stated probe, and the round with an observed probe. Flag any trait with only a stated probe as a known risk so interviewers press harder there.

### Step 3: Choose question techniques

Read `references/question-toolbox.md` for the full set of indirect and cross-examination techniques (timeline pinning, cost pinning, reverse inquiry, projective, bracketing, false-premise traps, withheld information, planted errors, role-play, decision drills, live tool review), example questions per trait, and the "I don't know" recovery ladder. Prefer questions whose honest answer is easier than the rehearsed one.

### Step 4: Design rounds within the time budget

Read `references/round-design.md` for round ordering, timing tables, standard calendar slots, delegation to HR, online execution, and speed mechanics. Key rules:

- Fit rounds to the total budget (including scorecards and debrief), not the other way round. Two hours of hiring-team time per candidate is enough when logistics are delegated.
- Every round gets a minute-by-minute timing table, assigned signals, strong/weak listen-fors for each question, and one skip-if-short block.
- Use standard calendar lengths (10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60 min). Round up rather than cut a probe, and say so when rounding changes the total.
- Place round-specific guidance (trap rules, recovery ladder, role-play prep) inside the round where it's used, not in a general preamble.

### Step 5: Scoring and decisions

Read `references/scoring-and-decisions.md` for the 1–4 scale, how to write behavioral anchors, decision rules (veto, gates, hire bar, domain-gap hires, follow-ups), the debrief agenda, scorecard template and calibration loop.

### Step 6: Retention, potential and speed

Build these in rather than bolting them on (details in `references/round-design.md` and `references/question-toolbox.md`):

- **Retention:** motivation match ("more of / less of" vs. what the role offers), pattern of moves, a realistic job preview in every round, values fit.
- **Potential:** learning velocity, observed coachability (give one hint and watch), self-improvement unasked, transfer, ambition with a plan in motion.
- **Speed:** pre-booked interviewer holds, same-day go/no-go, debrief within 24 hours, references in parallel with the final round, offer approvals agreed in advance, a first-contact-to-offer target, a note to the candidate after every round.

## Outputs

- **Interviewer guide** (the usual deliverable): overview with schedule table and coverage table; rules and scoring with anchors and decision rules; one section per round with timing table, scored signals, scripted questions, follow-ups, strong/weak listen-fors and go/no-go; scorecard template; debrief agenda; HR responsibilities with scripts. Make it a shareable document, not a chat reply, when the user wants something to keep or hand to interviewers.
- **Shorter variants** when asked to compress: keep the gates and the observed probes, cut stated probes that duplicate coverage, show what was cut and its cost, and keep each value covered at least once.
- **Question banks** for specific traits: technique, question, follow-ups, what strong and weak sound like.
- **General summaries/playbooks** when asked for something role-agnostic: principles, steps, toolbox, round design, scoring, checklist (`references/checklist.md`).

## Guardrails

- **Fairness and legality.** Never suggest questions about age, family, marital status, pregnancy, health, religion, nationality or other protected characteristics, even indirectly. If a candidate volunteers such information, don't follow up or record it. Ask work authorization only as a plain logistics question.
- **Compensation.** Leave salary out of screens and guides unless the user asks for it.
- **Traps are for observation, not humiliation.** One or two per session; never reveal one the candidate fell for; acknowledge one they caught warmly; reveal role-play setups at the close.
- **Recruiting/HR must not coach candidates.** They shouldn't describe the team's values or what interviewers look for.
- **AI-assistance in online interviews.** Design probes that need live, personal answers (timeline pinning, withheld info, sketching, role-play). Treat suspicious patterns as observations, never conclusions.
- **Don't invent facts about the user's team, role or company.** Use placeholders like [location] or [X] days.
